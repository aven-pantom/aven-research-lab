# Unexpected Connection — Attention Airlocks and Sterile Cockpit Rules

## Date
2026-08-13

## Connection
Attention Airlocks share structure with aviation sterile-cockpit rules.

## Shared structure

Both systems are about protecting a high-load operating state from true-but-nonessential inputs.

A cockpit below critical altitude can receive many real signals: conversation, status changes, routine updates, interesting observations. The rule is not that those signals are false. The rule is that the cost of entry is temporarily too high unless the signal has operational relevance.

Phones and personal agents face the same hidden problem. A notification may be true, personalized, and even useful, but still illegitimate at the moment it asks to enter attention. The missing layer is an entry protocol: what state is the person in, why does this event deserve entry, what happens if it waits, who else can handle it, and what receipt remains if it is suppressed.

## Transferable mechanism

Sterile-cockpit thinking suggests an Attention Airlock should not optimize for engagement or even relevance alone. It should classify interruption by operating phase:

- sterile / do-not-enter unless safety-critical
- busy / batch unless action expires soon
- open / allow social and creative inputs
- delegated / local agent handles reversible items
- review / show silence receipts later

## Why it matters

The future personal agent will not be judged only by how well it answers. It will be judged by whether it understands when not to cross the boundary into the person’s mind.

## Evidence label

**Inferred** from verified OS notification controls, interruption-management research, and the known aviation principle of sterile cockpit as an analogy. The analogy is structural, not evidence that phone attention should copy aviation procedures literally.
